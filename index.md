## Welcome

Welcome to my NSF DREAM research website! This site documents my work as a Master's Research Apprentice through the Distributed Research Experiences for Advanced Master's (DREAM) program.

Over the course of the project, I investigated how dating platforms approach, design, and communicate about user safety. What started as an exploration of how safety features could be directly tested evolved into a study of Tinder's publicly documented safety ecosystem and the ways individual features connect to different risks, problem areas, and approaches to protecting users.

This site captures that process from beginning to end: the literature that shaped my thinking, early feature inventories and testing plans, the methodological pivots that changed the direction of the project, the framework I developed for analyzing dating-platform safety features, and the findings that came out of the final study.

You can explore the project itself, follow along with my research journal, or jump straight to the final report below.

## Where I Took Inspiration

Research projects don't appear out of nowhere! A few papers and projects especially shaped how I thought about platform safety, what counts as a "safety feature," and how these systems might be studied.

### Safety Features Should Be Testable

**_Broken, Buried, or Missing: Anatomies of Failure (and Success) of Social Media Child Safety Features_**

This work showed me what a systematic safety-feature audit could look like. Rather than taking platforms' descriptions of their safety tools at face value, the researchers evaluated whether features actually functioned as described and whether users could realistically access them. That distinction between what a platform says a feature does and what can actually be evaluated became an important starting point for my own project.

### Turning Safety Features Into a Framework

**_Analyzing Social Media Claims regarding Youth Online Safety Features to Identify Problem Areas and Communication Gaps_**

This paper became especially influential once my project shifted toward analyzing public platform documentation. The researchers systematically analyzed hundreds of platform communications, identified the safety features described within them, and mapped those features to problem areas and online risks. Their approach gave me a foundation that I could adapt from youth social media safety to the very different context of online dating.

### Safety Isn't Neutral

**_Individualized Responsibility, Policing, and Surveillance: Examining the Carceral Logic in Dating App Companies' Approaches to Safety_**

This paper pushed me to think beyond whether a safety feature simply exists. Its analysis of dating-app safety through individual responsibility, policing, and surveillance raised questions about what platforms mean when they call something "safe," who is expected to manage risk, and what users may be asked to give up in exchange for protection.

### Dating Apps Are Their Own Kind of Platform

**_Online Dating Platform Safeguards and Self-Protection: How Dating Platforms Characterise, Respond to, and Safeguard Against Harms_**

Dating apps aren't just social media with swiping. This work helped ground the project in the particular challenges of online dating, especially the movement from online interaction to offline encounters and the tension between platform responsibility and users being expected to protect themselves.

### What If We Designed Around "Yes"?

**_Yes: Affirmative Consent as a Theoretical Framework for Understanding and Imagining Social Platforms_**

This one influenced how I thought about agency more broadly. The paper treats affirmative consent as something that can inform the design of social technologies, not only interpersonal relationships. Its principles of consent being voluntary, informed, revertible, specific, and unburdensome gave me another lens for thinking about what meaningful user control might look like in social and dating systems.
