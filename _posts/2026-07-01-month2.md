July marked a major shift in the direction of the project. After spending the previous few weeks to a month identifying safety features, developing a taxonomy, and preparing controlled accounts and testing scenarios, we began setting up the accounts needed to conduct direct platform testing. During this process, however, we encountered new photo and identity verification requirements that made the original testing approach difficult to carry out reliably.

Rather than beginning testing as planned, much of this month became an exercise in understanding that constraint and redesigning the research approach around it.

## Activities

- Worked with an undergraduate researcher to begin setting up the controlled accounts developed during the previous phase of the project.
- Encountered photo and identity verification requirements during account creation that prevented us from creating the set of controlled accounts required for the planned testing scenarios.
- Spent approximately a week investigating whether there were alternative, methodologically appropriate ways to conduct the planned account-based testing.
- Determined that the account-testing approach was not practical under the current platform constraints.
- Met with my advisor to reconsider the project's scope and identify research questions that could still be evaluated systematically without relying on controlled account testing.
- Returned to the literature to examine how previous researchers have studied, categorized, and audited platform safety features.
- Reviewed *Analyzing Social Media Claims regarding Youth Online Safety Features to Identify Problem Areas and Communication Gaps*, recommended by my advisor, as a potential methodological reference for the revised project.
- Began developing a document-based approach centered on systematically identifying and analyzing platforms' publicly documented safety features.

## Findings

The most significant finding this month was methodological rather than about a particular safety feature. The ability to audit a platform is partly determined by the platform's own access and verification infrastructure. Our original methodology assumed that we could create controlled accounts with different characteristics and use them to reproduce specific interactions. New verification requirements challenged that assumption.

After exploring alternatives, we decided not to build the research around attempting to bypass these systems. Instead, the constraint prompted a broader reconsideration of what evidence could be collected systematically and reproducibly.

The literature review provided a useful alternative. In *Analyzing Social Media Claims regarding Youth Online Safety Features to Identify Problem Areas and Communication Gaps*, Ma et al. systematically analyzed official platform communications to identify and categorize safety features across social media platforms. Their methodology uses sources such as official press releases and Help or Safety Center materials, then standardizes the safety features described across those sources and maps them to established categories of online risk.

This approach helped demonstrate that platform documentation could itself serve as a meaningful source of evidence. It also aligned with work I had already begun during the first month, when I was using safety centers, help documentation, and platform announcements to build the initial feature inventory.

## Challenges

The largest challenge this month was accepting that a substantial part of the original research plan was no longer feasible. We had already invested time in designing account configurations, identifying testing prerequisites, and mapping features to controlled testing scenarios.

The verification barrier also raised a larger methodological question: how can independent researchers systematically evaluate platform safety systems when access to those systems increasingly depends on identity, device, or biometric verification?

Changing direction required distinguishing the underlying research goal from the particular method originally chosen to study it. The goal remained understanding how dating platforms approach user safety. The challenge was developing a new methodology that could investigate that question using evidence that could be collected consistently across platforms.

## Next Steps

- Define the revised research scope around systematic analysis of publicly documented dating-platform safety features.
- Establish which official platform sources should be included in the analysis.
- Develop consistent inclusion and exclusion criteria for identifying safety features.
- Adapt existing online-safety risk frameworks to the specific risks and interactions present on dating platforms.
- Refine the feature taxonomy using the revised methodology.
- Begin systematically collecting and coding platform safety documentation.
- Document the methodological change and its implications for the remainder of the project.
