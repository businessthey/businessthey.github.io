September, the final month of the project, focused on narrowing the research scope and conducting a deeper analysis of Tinder's safety ecosystem. After rebuilding the data collection process in August, it became clear that conducting the same level of analysis across Tinder, Bumble, and Grindr would be difficult within the remaining project timeline.

With support from the undergraduate research assistant, I initially narrowed the scope to Tinder and Grindr before ultimately focusing the remainder of the project on Tinder. This allowed me to move beyond collecting documentation and spend more time understanding the safety features, platform communications, and methodological questions within a single platform.

## Activities

- Reassessed the project scope based on the size of the collected dataset and the remaining research timeline.
- Initially narrowed the analysis from Tinder, Bumble, and Grindr to Tinder and Grindr.
- Further narrowed the final analysis to Tinder to allow for a more comprehensive review of one platform.
- Re-ran the scraping process in September to collect the most up-to-date version of Tinder's public Help/Support and newsroom materials.
- Reviewed the full Tinder corpus and re-identified safety features using the revised dataset and methodology.
- Identified additional safety features that had not appeared in the original feature inventory.
- Reviewed the documentation associated with each feature to better understand how Tinder describes its availability, operation, intended purpose, and relationship to user safety.
- Continued reviewing literature on dating-app safety, consent, platform interventions, and approaches to evaluating safety systems.
- Reached out to researchers and mentors to better understand the broader research landscape and identify related work that could inform the next phase of the project.
- Reviewed work on affirmative consent in online dating, including research by Im et al., which helped broaden how I was thinking about safety beyond reactive features and toward how platforms might support safer interactions through design.
- Began thinking beyond feature identification toward qualitative analysis of the language Tinder uses to communicate about its safety systems.
- Reached out to research mentors to discuss reflexive thematic analysis (RTA) and approaches for analyzing quotations collected from platform documentation.
- Began working with the undergraduate research assistant on qualitative coding of the collected materials.
- Revisited the possibility of controlled account testing as a potential next phase of the research and investigated whether the verification barriers encountered earlier in the summer could be addressed.

## Findings

Narrowing the project to Tinder made it possible to examine the platform's safety ecosystem at a much deeper level. Rather than treating the feature inventory as a static list, I returned to the full corpus and re-identified features from the collected documentation. This process surfaced features that had not appeared during the initial manual sourcing process and reinforced the value of beginning with a broader corpus rather than searching only for features already known to exist.

The deeper review also highlighted that identifying a feature is only one part of understanding a platform's approach to safety. Individual documents contain claims about why features exist, how they operate, who can access them, what risks they are intended to address, and what impact the platform claims they have. These quotations provide another potential layer of analysis beyond the feature taxonomy itself.

At the same time, returning to the literature helped recontextualize what future work could look like. Research on affirmative consent and other approaches to dating-app safety pushed me to think beyond evaluating whether existing safety features function as described. It raised broader questions about where platforms intervene, whether safety systems primarily respond to harm after it occurs, and how platform design might instead support safer interactions earlier in the user journey.

This literature review, along with conversations with researchers and mentors working in related areas, helped situate the feature audit within a broader landscape of dating-app safety research and identify directions that could extend beyond the current project.

I also began exploring reflexive thematic analysis as a possible approach for analyzing platform communications. I spoke with research mentors about how RTA could be applied to the collected quotations and what kinds of research questions the qualitative analysis could reasonably answer.

Finally, the project returned to one of the challenges encountered earlier in the summer: direct platform testing. Although verification requirements prevented the original controlled-account methodology, testing documented features in practice remains an important potential extension of the work.

## Next Steps

Although the DREAM summer research period is ending, several directions remain for continuing the work:

- Continue refining and validating the Tinder safety-feature inventory.
- Complete qualitative coding of relevant platform documentation and quotations.
- Further develop the approach for using reflexive thematic analysis to examine Tinder's safety communications.
- Continue reviewing research on consent, safety interventions, and dating-platform design to contextualize the identified features and inform future research questions.
- Investigate not only how platforms respond to harmful interactions, but where product design may support prevention, boundary-setting, and consent before harm occurs.
- Investigate whether a reproducible and methodologically appropriate approach to controlled account testing is possible under current verification requirements.
- Compare Tinder's documented safety features with their behavior in practice if controlled testing becomes feasible.
- Apply the refined document-analysis methodology to Grindr, Bumble, and other dating platforms in future work.
