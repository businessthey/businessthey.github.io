This week focused on building an initial inventory of safety features and developing a framework for categorizing them. As I began systematically reviewing platforms, I found that methodological decisions around sourcing, categorization, and platform selection were just as important as the features themselves. I also had the opportunity to begin collaborating with another student interested in this area of research, which provided valuable lessons in communication, coordination, and shared problem-solving.

## Activities

- Began sourcing and documenting safety features across major dating platforms.
- Developed an initial categorization framework for organizing safety features.
- Reviewed safety centers, help documentation, and platform-specific safety resources.
- Discussed project methodology, feature definitions, and categorization approaches with collaborators.
- Began working with an undergraduate researcher interested in online safety and platform governance.

## Findings

This week marked the transition from understanding the literature to beginning feature sourcing and categorization. As I reviewed safety documentation and premium feature offerings across platforms, I began developing an initial taxonomy for organizing safety-related features and interventions.

One of the most interesting early observations was that some privacy and safety-related capabilities are embedded within premium subscription tiers rather than being universally available to all users. For example, premium offerings on Tinder and Bumble include features such as Incognito Mode, which allows users to limit profile visibility, and filtering capabilities that enable users to prioritize interactions with verified profiles. Some premium features also allow users to express interest before a mutual match occurs, such as Tinder's First Impressions feature.

These observations raised questions about the relationship between monetization and safety. While these features are often marketed as convenience or premium experience enhancements, they can also function as privacy and risk-management tools. This prompted me to think more critically about whether certain forms of safety and privacy are being distributed unevenly across users based on willingness or ability to pay.

At the same time, themes from *Individualized Responsibility, Policing, and Surveillance: Examining the Carceral Logic in Dating App Companies' Approaches to Safety* surfaced repeatedly throughout the sourcing process. Many platform safety interventions rely on increased verification, identity confirmation, monitoring, or other forms of user scrutiny. This raised additional questions about how platforms define "safe" users and the extent to which safety mechanisms may be intertwined with surveillance-oriented approaches to risk management.

Finally, I received feedback to narrow the scope of the project to Tinder and Bumble. Although I remain interested in examining Grindr due to the unique safety considerations and experiences of LGBTQ+ users, focusing on Tinder and Bumble will allow me to establish a more reproducible feature-sourcing methodology before expanding to additional platforms. This was an important lesson in balancing research ambition with methodological rigor.
